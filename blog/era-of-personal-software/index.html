The Era of Personal Software Is Here

October 20, 2026

I used Claude to take my TV's home screen apart and rebuild it for my family: no ads, our apps in one row, photos we chose, and the day's prayer times. This is what changed, how you can do the same, and the risk that comes with it.

My TV opened with an advert. Before I could get to Netflix, there was a sponsored banner for a motorbike, a row “Sponsored by JioHotstar”, and a Xiaomi home screen I never asked for. The TV is a small Android computer, and like most computers it does what its maker wants first.

So I opened Claude Code on my laptop and asked it to help me take back the home screen. It found the TV on my Wi-Fi, made a backup, and switched off the parts I didn’t want. Then we built the parts I did want. It took one evening and one morning, and I wrote almost none of the code myself.

What we changed, in order

From a billboard to our TV

  1. Found the TV and backed it up

    With USB debugging on, the TV accepts commands over Wi-Fi. Claude found it, saved the list of apps and every setting, and wrote a restore script before touching anything.

  2. Switched off 33 apps

    Tracking, sponsored rows, Xiaomi's PatchWall, partner auto-installs and apps nobody used. They are disabled, not deleted: one command brings them all back.

  3. Replaced the home screen

    I copied the open-source cleanyourtv launcher and set it up with our eight apps in one row. You can do the same, copy mine, or just ask Claude to build a launcher the way you want it.

  4. Added wallpapers that change

    A folder of photos changes behind Home every 30 minutes. A Wallpaper tile opens a picker, so anyone can choose one with the remote.

  5. Added prayer times

    Calculated on the TV, with no internet: Bengaluru, Karachi method, Hanafi Asr, plus the Hijri date. The current and next prayer sit under the clock, a tile opens the full day, and the screensaver shows it over the photos.

Why this matters

Software for an audience of one

For most of my life, software was something you bought. If a product did something you didn’t like, you lived with it, complained, or switched brands. Changing it meant being a developer with a free weekend.

That has changed. I didn’t need to know how Android launchers work, or which of 124 packages on my TV were safe to switch off. Claude read the TV’s logs, ran the commands, built the app, installed it, and checked every step with a screenshot from the TV. My job was to decide: keep the voice remote, keep casting, show these eight apps, use these photos, calculate prayer times the way my family prays.

I think this is what personal software means. You can open almost anything you own, keep the parts you like, and replace the rest with something made only for you. Nobody would sell a launcher with Hanafi Asr times for Bengaluru on the screensaver. Now nobody has to.

Do it yourself

All you need is this

You need an Android TV or Google TV, a computer on the same Wi-Fi, and Claude Code.

  1. Turn on USB debugging on the TV

    Settings → Device Preferences → About → press OK on Build seven times. Go back to Developer options and turn on USB debugging.

  2. Open Claude Code in an empty folder

    On your computer, on the same Wi-Fi as the TV.

  3. Paste the prompt below, with your TV model

    Claude asks before it changes anything you might use.

  4. Accept "Allow USB debugging?" on the TV

    Tick "Always allow from this computer", then answer Claude's questions about your apps.

  5. Turn USB debugging off when you're done

    The new home screen keeps working without it.

You don’t have to copy anyone’s launcher: Claude can build one from scratch with exactly the settings you want. Starting from cleanyourtv and its full prompt gets you more for free: bug fixes, translations, and app lists that other people have tested on 66 TV models.

Everyone is doing this

763 forks in three days

The cleanyourtv repo went up on 17 October 2026. Three days later it had about 6,400 stars and 763 forks. When I looked through the forks, 92 of them had changed the launcher in their own way:

  • manojm321/jain-tv-launcherA Jain calendar on the home screen, calculated offline
  • majnioui/CleanXiaomiTVA Prayer Times screen with the Hijri date, the idea behind mine
  • gpokhark/cleanyourtvA "Last on YouTube" row of recently watched videos
  • wevbert/cleanyourtvA Netflix data limiter and a PlayStation tile

Each one is somebody’s TV, made for their house. That is the part I find exciting.

The catch

The same superpower works for attackers

AI made it cheap to fork a project, change it and send the change back. That is why one small repo has hundreds of forks and a steady stream of contributions. It also makes the supply chain wider than anyone can read.

The home screen is the first app your TV runs, every time. Mine can read the photos folder and knows which Wi-Fi it’s on. If someone slips harmful code into a popular repo, everyone who pulls the latest version and installs it puts that code on their TV. I merged 83 commits from the original repo without reading every line before I stopped to think about this.

What I do now

  • Build from my own fork. Nothing updates on its own; the TV only changes when I build and install.
  • Don't sync blindly. If I want something new from the original, I read that change, or ask Claude to review it, and copy only that part.
  • Check permissions. My launcher has no internet permission, so it can't send data out by itself.
  • Keep changes small. A focused change is easy to read; a big merge is not.
  • Close the door afterwards. USB debugging goes off when I'm done.
